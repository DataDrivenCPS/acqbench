Verdict. ums-timescale-fix (drops the four partial indexes on timeseries, keeps the unique (ref_uri, ts) index) is a strict win on this workload: 1.44× faster ingest (30.4 → 21.1 min for 136.8M rows, 75k → 108k rows/s), 1.82× smaller on disk (66.5 → 36.6 GB; index bytes 52.6 → 22.7 GB, table data unchanged at 13.9 GB), and no query got slower: median p50 ratio fix ÷ main = 0.94, i.e. ~5% faster across the 30 queries, with the biggest gains on the small "latest N" reads (0.73–0.80×). None of the 30 GET /timeseries shapes ever used the dropped indexes — postgres served everything from the unique (ref_uri, ts) index, which is why removing them costs nothing on reads.
Where the remaining cost is. Postgres still runs at ~0.9 cores for the whole ingest with the acquirium process idle (0.1 cores), so the upsert path is still the bottleneck; it is now paying for one 21.5 GB unique index plus Timescale's automatic 1.3 GB timeseries_ts_idx instead of six indexes. DuckDB on the same main remains 5.4× faster to ingest (586k rows/s) and 5× smaller (7.3 GB) than even the fixed Timescale, and still 0 of 12 chunks are compressed (the 7-day compression policy never fires on a fresh load).
Read-side picture is unchanged. Both Timescale runs keep their 2–3× lead over DuckDB on point / small-window reads (~1.5 ms vs DuckDB's ~4 ms floor) and on the LIMIT shapes DuckDB does not push down ("30 days limit 10000": 9.5 vs 118 ms; "latest 1 desc": 1.6 vs 16 ms), while DuckDB stays ~25–30% faster on the ≥ 200k-row scans (P1 full: 325 vs 455 ms). Cold first-touch reads on Timescale are ~150–250 ms dearer than warm on the large streams, slightly less so on the fix (smaller working set).
Caveat on absolute numbers. Today's main/timescale scans are ~15% slower than the 2026-08-26 run of the same schema (P1 full: 484 vs 408 ms) while main/duckdb reproduced to within 3% (325 vs 317 ms); the VM host was carrying another user's container load during these runs. The main-vs-fix Timescale runs were back-to-back on the same host state, so their ratio is the trustworthy number, not the absolute latencies.
